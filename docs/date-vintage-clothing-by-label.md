# How to Date Vintage Clothing from Its Label

To date a vintage piece from its label, read four things together: the RN or CA number, which fixes the earliest year the maker could have used that label; the country of manufacture, which rules out years for countries that have since changed name; the care wording or symbols, compulsory in the United States from 1972 and symbols only from 1997; and the fibre names, where trade names such as Crimplene belong to a decade while generic names mostly say which side of the Atlantic the label was written for. On the 701 labels we have photographed and read on our own pieces (346 in stock, the rest sold), 127 carry an RN or CA number, 293 name a country and 188 carry care wording. None of these is a proof on its own; together they narrow a piece to a decade.

Everything below is counted from those labels and rebuilds itself as pieces are listed or sold. Where a rule comes from outside our own rail, the reference is linked. Updated 21 November 2026 (newest label reading). Decades on our listings with a read label: Y2K 198 · 90s 72 · 80s 12 · 70s 5.

## RN number lookup: RN and CA numbers on the label, and the earliest year they date

An **RN** (Registered Identification Number) is issued by the US Federal Trade Commission to a maker or importer who would rather not print its name on the label; a **CA number** is the Canadian equivalent. A company receives its number once and keeps it, so the number tells you the earliest year a label could carry it, never the year the garment was made. By the collectors' rule of thumb the present RN series started around 1959 at about 13670; numbers below that are likely older. The [FTC RN registry search](https://rn.ftc.gov/Account/BasicSearch), free, tells you which company holds a number, which is what we check first; it does not give the year. Without the registry, the [Vintage Fashion Guild forum: a way to date RN numbers](https://forums.vintagefashionguild.org/threads/a-way-to-date-rn-numbers-is-this-correct.23527/) is a rule of thumb: subtract 13670, divide by about 2,189 registrations a year, add to 1959. The "earliest year" column below uses that method and is a guide, not a proof.

To look a number up yourself, type it into the [FTC RN registry](https://rn.ftc.gov/Account/BasicSearch): it returns the company the number is registered to, which is the first thing to compare with the brand on the label. The year is not in the registry; the table below estimates it from the number itself.

## "Made in": countries that date themselves

A country of manufacture rarely gives a year, but some names stop or start at a known date. "Made in Hong Kong" is typical of the 1960s to the 1980s, and after the [Handover of Hong Kong, 1 July 1997](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handover_of_Hong_Kong) labels moved towards "China" or "Hong Kong SAR", without the old wording ever vanishing. "Made in the EU" cannot be older than the [Maastricht Treaty in force, 1 November 1993](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maastricht_Treaty). West Germany ends with [German reunification, 3 October 1990](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_reunification), Czechoslovakia with its [Dissolution of Czechoslovakia, 31 December 1992](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_Czechoslovakia), Yugoslavia with the [Breakup of Yugoslavia, from 1991](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakup_of_Yugoslavia), the USSR with its [Dissolution of the Soviet Union, 26 December 1991](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolution_of_the_Soviet_Union): a label naming any of them is a piece from before that date. Each is a "not after" or "not before", and a reprinted label can lie, so again a guide, not a proof.

## Care labels: wording, temperatures, symbols

In the United States a permanent care label has been compulsory since the [FTC on the Care Labeling Rule (issued December 1971)](https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/cases/1999/03/ftc.gov-hilfiger2.htm) came into force in 1972, so an American-labelled piece with no care wording at all is either earlier than that or outside the rule (leather, suede, fur). The rule asked for words; [FTC: care symbols allowed from July 1997](https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/federal_register_notices/concerning-trade-regulation-rule-care-labeling-textile-wearing-apparel-and-certain-piece-goods/970206carelabelingoftextile.pdf), so a US label with symbols only is 1997 or later. In Europe the symbols are the [GINETEX, the care symbols used in Europe](https://www.ginetex.net/) set, in use since the 1960s, and care wording has always been voluntary: what is compulsory there is the fibre content ([EU Regulation 1007/2011 on textile fibre names](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2011/1007/oj)). Two habits that help place a label: temperatures written in degrees ("wash at 30°") tend to be European; "do not bleach" and "tumble dry low" spelled out as words tend to be American.

## Fibre names as a clock

Trade names are the sharpest clue a label offers. Terylene is ICI's polyester, in bulk production from 1955; [Crimplene (ICI, launched 1959)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crimplene) is its textured version, the fabric of the 1960s A-line dress, out of fashion by the mid-1970s; [Lyocell / Tencel (commercial from 1992)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyocell) cannot appear on a label before the 1990s. Generic names date less and place more: EU labels say "viscose", "elastane" and "polyamide" where American labels say "rayon", "spandex" and "nylon". Stretch denim appeared around 1980 and became common in the late 1990s, so "elastane" on a pair of jeans points to the 1980s or later, most often Y2K.

## How the label itself is made

- A woven label with the brand name and a separate woven or printed care tag behind it is the usual form from the 1970s to today; a brand label with no care tag at all, on a piece that is not leather or fur, points to before 1972 in the US or to a European maker who never added one.
- Printed, heat-transferred "tagless" labels inside the neck are a 2000s habit; a size tag that lists several countries' sizes at once (UK, EU, US) is mostly a 1990s-onwards habit.
- Numbers that look like RNs on a European label are often style or batch numbers, not registrations: if the [FTC RN registry search](https://rn.ftc.gov/Account/BasicSearch) returns nothing, do not force a year out of it.
- The size on the label is the last thing to trust for the date; sizes drifted by decade, which is why we list the [measured size](https://kitanesque.co.uk/size-guide/) on every piece.

## Questions about dating labels

### Does an RN number prove a piece is vintage?

No. An RN number is a registration the US Federal Trade Commission issues to a company once, and the company keeps it for as long as it trades, so the number fixes the earliest year a label could have been made, never the year it was made. CA 25594, registered to Diesel Canada Inc., appears on 6 different pieces of ours. On our own labels, 127 pieces carry an RN or CA number (182 numbers from 139 registrants), and the same number turns up on pieces listed as different decades.

### What does "Made in Hong Kong" tell me about the date?

That the piece most likely pre-dates the late 1990s. Hong Kong was a major garment exporter from the 1960s to the 1980s; after the handover of 1 July 1997 labels increasingly read "Made in China" or "Hong Kong SAR", though "Made in Hong Kong" never disappeared, so treat it as a guide, not a proof. 4 of the 293 country labels we have read say Hong Kong.

### Why does my 1970s jacket have no care label?

Because care labels only became compulsory in the United States with the FTC Care Labeling Rule, issued in December 1971 and in force from 1972, and in the UK and Europe they have always been voluntary: fibre content is the legal requirement there, care wording is a courtesy. Leather, fur and suede are outside the US rule as well. On our pieces, 188 of 701 labels carry care wording, and it is the European habit of writing temperatures ("wash at 30°", 21 of ours) that often gives away where a piece was labelled.

### Can I date a piece from the fibre name on the label?

Sometimes. Trade names belong to decades: Terylene is ICI polyester from the mid-1950s and Crimplene, its textured cousin, was launched in 1959 and was out of fashion by the mid-1970s; lyocell (Tencel) only exists on labels from 1992. Generic names tell you where a label was written for rather than when: "viscose", "elastane" and "polyamide" are the EU words, "rayon", "spandex" and "nylon" the American ones. On our labels, elastane appears 73 times, almost all on Y2K stretch denim, viscose 29 times against rayon 8, lyocell 2.

### How accurate is the "RN formula" for the year?

Rough. The community method subtracts 13670 (by the collectors' reading, the first number of the 1959 scheme) from the RN, divides by the average number of registrations a year (about 2,189) and adds the result to 1959; that gives the earliest plausible year to within a few years. The FTC RN registry search, free, tells you which company holds the number, which is what we check first; it does not give the year. Either way the answer is "not before", never "made in".
